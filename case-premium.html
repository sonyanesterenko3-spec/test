01 · Overview

15 people clicked "Apply" out of 16,279
who visited the page

The Premium section of UKRSIBBANK's website was built for one of the bank's most valuable audience segments — yet it was failing them completely. The page looked identical to every other section of the site. Users couldn't find the benefits. They couldn't compare cards. They couldn't even find the application form.

In three months, only 15 people out of 16,279 visitors clicked "Fill out an application." The form was buried so deep that only 10% of users ever scrolled to it. This wasn't a conversion problem — it was a fundamental UX and information architecture breakdown.

I joined as part of the Idea Lab initiative to run the full research-to-redesign cycle: competitive analysis, heatmap review, usability testing with 18 premium clients, hypothesis generation, redesign, and a second round of testing with 7 additional users to validate the new design.

This project was not shipped to production. After completing research, redesign, and validation testing — all showing strong results — I transitioned to another team initiative and handed off the work to other designers. The value of this case is in the depth of research process and the measurable improvement the new design achieved during testing.

03 · Competitor Analysis

Every competitor signals premium
through darkness and restraint

I mapped the premium sections of all major Ukrainian banks and monobank. The pattern was clear: PrivatBank, Oschadbank, and Raiffeisen all shifted to dark, high-contrast visual languages for their premium tiers — visually separating themselves from their standard offering. monobank's Platinum page was the strongest example: a black-first design with a clear product hierarchy and bold typography.

UKRSIBBANK's premium page, by contrast, was styled identically to the SME section, the corporate section, and the personal banking section. There was no visual signal that you had arrived somewhere special.

05 · Usability Testing · Round 1

18 premium clients.
4 tasks. Consistent failure.

I recruited 18 respondents from the premium segment across 7 Ukrainian cities — Kyiv, Odesa, Vinnytsia, Ternopil, Lviv, Dnipro, and Kryvyi Rih. Sessions were moderated and recorded. Each user was given 4–5 tasks and scored on completion rate, time on task, error rate, and Subjective Experience Questionnaire (SEQ) difficulty (1 = very easy, 7 = very hard).

The script opened with warm-up questions about their experience with the bank and their understanding of premium benefits. Most couldn't name a single concrete premium benefit before starting the tasks. This was the first signal that the problem wasn't just UI — it was a complete failure to communicate value proposition.

05a · Task 1

Find information about
premium banking benefits

14 out of 18 respondents failed to find the benefits section. Those who eventually found it spent more than 7 minutes searching. SEQ difficulty was rated 6/7. The section that should have been the emotional hook of the entire page — the reason to become a premium client — was effectively invisible.

User quote

"Benefits 1, 2, 3 should be listed — but here everything is scattered across the site. You have to walk around and spend time reading."

User quote

"I don't even understand the advantages of Premium myself."

User observation

Apple Pay and Google Pay were listed as "premium benefits" — users were confused and dismissive. "These are standard everywhere. Why is this a premium feature?"

User observation

Free cash withdrawal in Ukraine listed as a benefit — users expected this as a baseline, not a distinguishing premium feature.

05b · Task 2

Find card information
and compare them

12 out of 18 users could not identify the differences between the two main premium cards (Black Edition and All Inclusive De Luxe). Users who did find information spent an average of 8+ minutes and had to open multiple browser tabs to compare. The SEQ difficulty score for this task was 5/7.

  • No comparison mechanism. Users opened multiple tabs side-by-side to compare — a behavior that signals complete failure of the page's information architecture.
  • Benefits and privileges were separated. Users couldn't understand why card benefits were in one section and "additional privileges" were in another. "Why are they split? They're the same thing."
  • Pricing was incomprehensible. "Fees are completely unclear — what am I actually paying for?"
  • One respondent didn't know which of her two bank cards was premium. A current premium client couldn't identify her own card category.

05c · Task 3

Submit an application
for premium banking

Users who found the form quickly (1 minute) had only seen it earlier by accident during their browsing — not because the navigation guided them there. Users who hadn't noticed it previously searched in entirely the wrong places: premium banking submenu, Mastercard privileges section, contacts page, and the general "private individuals" section.

Critical data point

Only 10% of visitors scrolled to the form in the last month. Over 3 months, only 15 people clicked "Fill out application" out of 16,279 who visited the page.

Insight

The page title "Premium clients" confused current premium clients — they don't need to apply. The title was actively misleading about the purpose of the page.

05d · Visual & Design Findings

The design didn't feel premium.
Users said so directly.

Beyond navigation and content, users responded consistently to the visual design itself. The premium section looked identical to the SME and corporate sections — no visual hierarchy signaled exclusivity or prestige.

  • "The design feels like it's for small business, not premium." Users expected a distinctly different aesthetic from a premium banking page.
  • Photography misrepresented the audience. "I'm a woman, but I see a 60-year-old man in the image. Where am I?" Users need to see themselves reflected.
  • Font size caused frustration. Multiple users cited small type as irritating and hard to read.
  • The app screenshot in the visuals showed an old version of the mobile app. Users noticed. This damaged trust in the brand's attention to quality.
  • No dark mode. Several users expected it as a baseline for a modern premium product.
  • Card images were too small and low quality. "I want to see the cards bigger and in better quality." The cards are the hero product — they weren't treated as one.

06 · Hypotheses

Every problem had a testable
hypothesis behind it

Research findings were translated into structured hypotheses — each framed as a specific, testable belief about what would improve the experience. All were confirmed during round-one testing. The redesign was built directly on these hypotheses.

Hypothesis 01 · Hero Section · ✓ Confirmed

The hero screen should immediately communicate premium value and contain a CTA for quick application. The existing hero was a generic carousel with no premium messaging and no conversion path.

Hypothesis 02 · Benefits Block · ✓ Confirmed

Users need a clear, scannable benefits block visible without scrolling. The "Premium Banking" nav dropdown didn't communicate what premium actually meant — it just listed subcategories.

Hypothesis 03 · Card Comparison · ✓ Confirmed

Users cannot compare cards without an explicit comparison structure. Side-by-side cards with distinct benefits and a unified tariff table would eliminate the multi-tab behavior.

Hypothesis 04 · Form / CTA · ✓ Confirmed

The CTA should be accessible from every point of the page. A single form at the bottom — never seen by 90% of users — is not a conversion mechanism. It's a missed opportunity.

Hypothesis 05 · Visual Design · ✓ Confirmed

The premium section should visually differentiate from all other bank sections. A dark, premium aesthetic — consistent with how all major competitors treat their premium tier — would set the right expectations on arrival.

Hypothesis 06 · Secondary Blocks · ✓ Confirmed

Not all existing blocks are necessary for the premium segment. Currency rates and generic app ads dilute the premium focus. The branch block should stay, but filtered by "nearest to me."

07 · Redesign

One page. Dark. Scannable.
Every CTA visible.

The redesign followed a strict hierarchy based on research findings. The content structure became: Hero with CTA → Benefits → Personal Manager story → Card comparison with tariff table → CTA → Nearest branches. Everything else was removed or deprioritized.

The visual language shifted to dark — near-black backgrounds, purple-tinted card accents, high-contrast white typography. The premium page finally looked and felt different from every other section of the site.

09 · Outcomes & Learnings

What this project
taught me

This was a complete research-to-design cycle — and the process worked exactly as it should. Every design decision was traceable to a specific user behavior or quote. The heatmaps told us where people stopped. The usability tests told us why. The hypotheses told us what to fix. The validation tests confirmed it worked.

The fact that the redesign wasn't shipped doesn't diminish the research value. The findings were real, the metrics improved dramatically in testing, and the work was handed off with a complete research brief, prototype, and validated design for the incoming team to continue.

If I could do one thing differently: I would have pushed for a staged launch — even one single change like moving the CTA to the hero — to gather production data earlier while the full redesign was still in progress.

Data before
design
The 15/16,279 conversion stat was the most persuasive stakeholder argument I had. Real numbers change conversations. No amount of "the page looks bad" would have had the same impact
Premium =
distinct
Premium users don't want a slightly improved version of the standard page. They want to feel that the product was built for them specifically. Visual differentiation is not cosmetic — it communicates value before a word is read
CTA
